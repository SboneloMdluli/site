Probabilistic Graphical Models: Bayesian Networks, Markov Networks, and Markov Blankets

February 22, 2026
Machine Learning

Probabilistic Graphical Models (PGMs) combine probability theory and graph theory to represent complex relationships among random variables. PGMs are attractive because they can encode conditional independence (for tractable inference) while remaining interpretable (you can literally “see” dependencies as edges in a graph).

From joint distributions to graphs

Let (X_1,\dots,X_n) be random variables. A fundamental identity used throughout PGMs is the chain rule:

$$ p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_1,\dots,x_{i-1}). $$

A graph (G=(V,E)) is a set of vertices (V) (variables) and edges (E) (relationships). Two common families show up in PGMs:

  • Directed graphs: edges have orientation (X \to Y).
  • Undirected graphs: edges have no orientation (X - Y).

In undirected models, the idea of a clique matters.

Clique: a set of vertices where every pair is connected by an edge. A clique is maximal if you cannot add any other vertex without breaking completeness.

Belief networks (Bayesian networks)

A Bayesian network (also called a belief network) is a directed acyclic graph (DAG). The key modeling move is replacing the generic chain rule factorization with a graph-respecting factorization:

$$ p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p!\left(x_i \mid \mathrm{Pa}(X_i)\right), $$

where (\mathrm{Pa}(X_i)) denotes the parents of node (X_i) in the DAG.

This expresses local conditional independences: each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents.

D-separation (how “independence” reads off the graph)

To talk precisely about conditional independence in a directed graph, we use d-separation. The classic three motifs are:

  1. Chain: (X \to Z \to Y) (conditioning on (Z) blocks the path)
  2. Fork: (X \leftarrow Z \to Y) (conditioning on (Z) blocks the path)
  3. Collider: (X \to Z \leftarrow Y) (conditioning on (Z) opens the path)

This is why Bayesian networks can be both interpretable (the motifs are easy to reason about) and efficient (many variables become irrelevant once you condition on the right ones).

Markov networks (Markov random fields)

A Markov network is an undirected probabilistic model. Instead of conditional probability tables along directed edges, we work with clique potentials.

Let (\mathcal{C}) denote the set of cliques in the graph. Then the joint distribution is written as:

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \psi_C(x_C), $$

where (\psi_C(\cdot)\ge 0) is a potential function over clique variables (x_C), and (Z) is the partition function (a normalizing constant that ensures probabilities sum to 1).

Markov networks are flexible because they do not enforce acyclicity and can be easier to specify for symmetric relationships. The trade-off is that they do not directly encode causal direction.

Learning PGMs: parameters vs structure

PGM “learning” typically means one (or both) of the following:

Parameter learning (probabilities with a fixed graph)

Assume the graph structure is known. Then:

  • In discrete Bayesian networks, parameters are often conditional probability tables (CPTs).
  • You can estimate parameters via maximum likelihood (count-and-normalize) or Bayesian estimation (e.g., Dirichlet priors).
  • With missing data, parameter estimation often becomes iterative (e.g., EM or Gibbs sampling).

Structure learning (discovering the graph)

When the structure is unknown, we want to infer which edges should exist.

  • Constraint-based methods use conditional independence tests (e.g., the PC algorithm).
  • Score-based methods search for high-scoring graphs under criteria like BIC, BDe, or MDL, usually with heuristic search (e.g., hill climbing, simulated annealing).
  • Hybrid methods try to combine the strengths of both (find a skeleton with tests, then refine with scoring).

The hard parts are computational: the number of possible graphs explodes with variables, “Markov equivalent” graphs can be indistinguishable without extra assumptions, and latent variables can confound dependencies.

Markov blankets (why you can ignore most variables)

The Markov blanket of a target variable (X) is the minimal set of variables that renders (X) conditionally independent of everything else in the model.

In a Bayesian network, the (Pearl) Markov blanket of (X) is:

  • Parents of (X)
  • Children of (X)
  • Co-parents of (X) (the other parents of (X)’s children)

If we write the Markov blanket as (\mathrm{MB}(X)), the key property is:

$$ X \perp!!!\perp \left(V \setminus \left({X}\cup \mathrm{MB}(X)\right)\right);\middle|; \mathrm{MB}(X). $$

Intuition

Everything you can learn about (X) from the rest of the network must flow through either:

  • what directly influences (X) (parents),
  • what (X) directly influences (children),
  • or alternative explanations for (X)’s children (co-parents).

Condition on those, and the rest of the graph becomes irrelevant for predicting or inferring (X).

Why Markov blankets matter in practice

  • Feature selection: focus only on the most relevant variables for predicting a target.
  • Faster inference: many queries reduce to local neighborhoods.
  • Interpretability: the blanket is a compact “explanation set” for what drives the target.

A simple example

Suppose your target is whether a patient has a condition (X). In a Bayesian network, plausible Markov blanket members might include:

  • Parents: risk factors that directly influence (X) (e.g., age, exposure variables)
  • Children: observed symptoms influenced by (X)
  • Co-parents: other causes of those symptoms (alternative explanations)

The Markov blanket idea says: to reason about (X), you don’t need “everything”—you need the smallest set that shields (X) from the rest.

Beyond Pearl: Friston blankets (briefly)

You’ll sometimes see Friston’s Markov blanket in neuroscience/active inference: it partitions states into internal vs external, connected through sensory and active boundary states. It’s related in spirit (a conditional-independence boundary), but it’s used for different modeling questions than the standard Bayesian-network Markov blanket above.

Closing thought

PGMs are powerful whenever you need more than point forecasts: they model full distributions, support updating under new information, and—via Markov blankets—help you scale to higher-dimensional systems without losing interpretability.
